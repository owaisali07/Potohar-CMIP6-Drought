# Abstract
The Potohar Plateau grows most of its wheat without irrigation, so its harvest depends on rain that falls in the cool season. Whether drought there will become more frequent under warming has not been tested with bias-corrected CMIP6 projections, an index chosen by its link to yields, and gauged river flow. We combined station records of the Pakistan Meteorological Department, gauged discharge of the Water and Power Development Authority, gridded observations for 1981–2024 and 27 downscaled CMIP6 models. The three-month Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index ending in December correlated best with district wheat yields (r = 0.60). The ten models with the most stable skill ranking project that drought months become 10.0 (SSP2-4.5) and 11.1 (SSP5-8.5) percentage points more frequent by 2076–2100, even though annual rainfall increases. The increase falls in winter and spring, when rainfall barely changes and evaporative demand rises. A seven-month drought that recurred about once in 33–50 years in 1985–2014 (depending on the estimator) recurs about once in 10 years by 2076–2100, and the drought exposure of rainfed wheat grows by 71% (95% CI 39–104%) under SSP5-8.5. Differences between models, not the scenario, dominate the uncertainty.
@@KEYWORDS Keywords: Drought, SPEI, CMIP6, Rainfed wheat, Copula return period, Potohar Plateau
# Introduction
Wheat is the main crop of the Potohar (Pothwar) Plateau in northern Punjab, and almost all of it is rainfed. The plateau covers the districts of Attock, Chakwal, Jhelum and Rawalpindi and adjoins Islamabad Capital Territory. It is drained mainly by the Soan River, and the summer monsoon brings most of its annual rain [@abbas2021]. The wheat crop, however, is sown in October–November and grows through winter and spring, when rain is scarce and variable. Droughts in this season reduce yields directly, and drought in the Soan basin propagates quickly from rainfall into river flow [@abbas2021; @sarwar2022].
Several studies have described drought on the plateau and in the surrounding basins. Abbas et al. [@abbas2021] and Sarwar et al. [@sarwar2022] characterised meteorological and hydrological droughts in the Soan basin and their propagation from gauge records. Goheer et al. [@goheer2023] mapped agricultural and meteorological drought risk in rainfed Potohar with satellite rainfall and vegetation data, and Moazzam et al. [@moazzam2022] combined CHIRPS rainfall, the Standardized Precipitation Index and climate projections for a scarcely gauged basin of northern Pakistan. For the plateau itself, the only drought projection used two regional climate models under the older RCP scenarios [@ain2020], and a recent CMIP6 study of its hydroclimate projected a warmer, wetter Potohar with more monsoon rain and less winter rain from two bias-corrected models, without assessing drought [@khan2024potohar].
Projections of drought under the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) of CMIP6 [@eyring2016] have so far been made at global or national scale. For Pakistan, Ullah et al. [@ullah2025] projected drought risk with 23 CMIP6 models under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 and at 1.5 and 2 °C of warming, and Khan [@khan2025] described the changing characteristics of 21st-century drought with a CMIP6 multimodel ensemble; neither study focused on the rainfed plateau or linked drought to its crops. Lindenlaub et al. [@lindenlaub2026] evaluated 18 CMIP6 models for agricultural drought and projected the six-month SPEI under three scenarios, with South Asia as a focus region. Yang et al. [@yang2026] showed that the SPI and the SPEI diverge under warming, because the SPEI also responds to rising evaporative demand. In a hot, semi-arid region such as the Potohar, this divergence decides whether a wetter future is also a less droughty one.
Four gaps remain for the plateau. First, no study has projected its drought with bias-corrected CMIP6 output under SSPs. Second, the index used for projections has not been chosen by its link to crop losses. Third, the propagation of drought from rainfall to river flow has not been tied to the gauged discharge of the Soan. Fourth, the exposure of rainfed crops to future drought has not been quantified at district level with its uncertainty.
Here we address these gaps for the four districts and Islamabad Capital Territory. We built an observed record for 1981–2024 from station data of the Pakistan Meteorological Department (PMD), gauged discharge of the Water and Power Development Authority (WAPDA) and gridded products. We chose the drought index and time scale from their correlation with official district wheat yields. We evaluated 27 NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 models [@thrasher2022], retained ten by a resampled skill ranking, corrected their remaining biases with quantile delta mapping [@cannon2015] and projected drought frequency, duration and severity for the near, mid and far future under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 and at three global warming levels. We then estimated joint duration–severity return periods with copulas and the exposure of rainfed wheat and groundnut in each district, and partitioned the projection uncertainty. The analysis plan for model selection and bias correction was fixed before the projections were run.
# Methodology
## Study area and spatial units
The study area is the four districts of the Potohar Plateau (Attock, Chakwal, Jhelum and Rawalpindi) and Islamabad Capital Territory (ICT), from the Common Operational Dataset of administrative boundaries of the United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (COD-AB, 2022) [@ochacod] (Fig. 1). Elevation rises from the plains along the Indus and Jhelum rivers to the Murree hills in the northeast. We delineated the Soan basin to the Dhok Pathan gauge and the catchment of the Chirah gauge from the Copernicus 30 m and 90 m digital elevation models [@copdem] with a D8 flow-direction algorithm. The delineated catchments differ from the published areas by −6.9% (Dhok Pathan) and −0.7% (Chirah). All gridded analyses use the native 0.25° grid of NEX-GDDP-CMIP6, the finest scale common to the projections and the observations; the 57 cells that intersect the five units or the basin form the analysis domain. Areas were computed on the sphere, and district and regional series are area-weighted means. Fig. 2 summarises the workflow of the study.

## Observational data
Daily precipitation, maximum temperature (Tmax) and minimum temperature (Tmin) for 1961–2010 were obtained from PMD for Islamabad and Murree. We merged them with the NOAA Global Surface Summary of the Day (GSOD) [@gsod] and GHCN-Daily [@menne2012] records of the same stations, with PMD values taking priority, and added every other GSOD/GHCN-Daily station within 100 km of the plateau (40 stations). PMD Murree matched GSOD Murree day by day, and PMD Islamabad matched GSOD Islamabad City (WMO 41577) better than the nearby Chaklala station, which we kept as a separate station. We set the PMD missing code (−99.9) to missing and trace rainfall (0.01 mm) to zero, removed values outside gross-error limits, and corrected one year (2004) of the Islamabad file in which the date labels skipped 29 February and repeated 31 December.
Gaps of up to 15 days were filled by month-wise empirical quantile mapping of co-located daily CHIRPS precipitation and ERA5-Land temperature, dew point and wind. The PMD files contain no humidity or wind; for Murree these came from GSOD, completed by quantile-mapped ERA5-Land, and for Islamabad from Chaklala, 11 km away in the same grid cell. A station was used if at least 70% of its months in 1985–2014 were complete (Supplementary Table 1). Gridded observations were CHIRPS v2.0 precipitation [@funk2015], ERA5-Land [@munoz2021] for the Penman–Monteith inputs, TerraClimate [@abatzoglou2018] and CRU TS 4.10 [@harris2020] as independent checks, and PERSIANN-CDR [@ashouri2015] for the spread of rainfall products. All were conservatively remapped to the 0.25° grid, and CHIRPS was also kept at 0.05° for observed trend maps.
Daily discharge at the Soan at Dhok Pathan (1964–2003), the Soan at Chirah (1962–2003) and the Sil River near Chahan (1962–2003) came from the Surface Water Hydrology Project of WAPDA. Because the gauge records end in 2003, we used GloFAS-ERA5 v4.0 daily discharge [@harrigan2020] for 1979–2024 at the grid cells of the delineated outlets, validated against the gauges. Official district wheat yields for 1985–2024 and groundnut yields for 2017–2024, with irrigated and un-irrigated (rainfed) areas, came from the Crop Reporting Service of Punjab, as published by the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, the Bureau of Statistics Punjab and the Agriculture Department Punjab; the Global Dataset of Historical Yields (GDHY) [@iizumi2020] served as a check. Further validation data were the MODIS NDVI and land-surface temperature [@didan2021; @wan2021], the C3S/ESA CCI soil moisture [@dorigo2017], SPEIbase v2.10 [@begueria2010] and the drought bulletins of the National Drought Monitoring Centre of PMD recovered from Internet Archive snapshots.
## Station quality control and homogeneity
We tested the annual series of each station for inhomogeneity with the standard normal homogeneity test (SNHT) [@alexandersson1986] and the Pettitt test [@pettitt1979] at the 5% significance level. Because the SNHT is designed as a relative test, we applied both tests to the ratio (precipitation, ET0) or difference (temperature) between the station and two independent references: CHIRPS and ERA5-Land at the station point, and TerraClimate at the station cell. A series was classed as inhomogeneous only when both tests rejected against both references, and such series were not used to evaluate models or gridded products. A station was also excluded from these comparisons when its elevation differed by more than 500 m from the mean elevation of its 0.25° cell, and station ET0 was compared only where at least 70% of the months had observed humidity and wind. Stations in the same 0.25° cell were averaged into one value.
## Reference evapotranspiration and drought indices
Reference evapotranspiration (ET0) was computed with the FAO-56 Penman–Monteith equation [@allen1998]:
@@EQ (1)|ET0 = [0.408 Δ (Rn − G) + γ (900 / (T + 273)) u2 (es − ea)] / [Δ + γ (1 + 0.34 u2)]
where R_n is net radiation, G the soil heat flux, T the mean air temperature, u_2 the wind speed at 2 m, e_s − e_a the vapour pressure deficit, Δ the slope of the saturation vapour pressure curve and γ the psychrometric constant. Ten-metre wind was converted to 2 m with the FAO-56 logarithmic profile. For gridded data we used ERA5-Land inputs (primary) and TerraClimate inputs (sensitivity), with G from the month-to-month change of temperature. Station solar radiation, which is not observed, was estimated from the temperature range, and the Hargreaves method was used only as a sensitivity test.
The Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI) [@mckee1993] fits a gamma distribution G_Γ to k-month precipitation totals x, with q the probability of zero precipitation:
@@EQ (2)|SPI = Φ⁻¹[ q + (1 − q) G(x) ]
The Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI) [@vicenteserrano2010] fits a three-parameter log-logistic distribution with scale a, shape b and origin c to the k-month water balance W = P − ET0:
@@EQ (3)|SPEI = Φ⁻¹[ F(D) ],   F(D) = [1 + (α / (D − γ))^β]⁻¹
and the standardized Reconnaissance Drought Index (RDIst) [@tsakiris2005] standardises the logarithm y_k of the ratio of k-month precipitation to ET0:
@@EQ (4)|RDIst = (ln(ΣP / ΣET0) − μ) / σ
where H is the cumulative probability of the k-month precipitation, Φ⁻¹ the inverse standard normal distribution, and ȳ and σ̂_y the mean and standard deviation of y_k. All three were computed for k = 1, 3, 6 and 12 months with unbiased probability-weighted moments and calibrated on 1985–2014 for the observations and for each model's own simulation; values beyond ±3 were capped. Hydrological drought was described by the Standardized Runoff Index (SRI) [@shukla2008] and the Streamflow Drought Index (SDI) [@nalbantis2009], and agricultural drought by the Vegetation Health Index (VHI) [@kogan1995] of MODIS over cropland. Months with SPI ≤ −1.5 and SPEI ≤ −1.5 were flagged as concurrent drought.
## Drought characteristics, trends and index selection
Drought events were identified by run theory [@yevjevich1967] with a threshold of −1. For an event of duration D months, severity and intensity are
@@EQ (5)|S = Σ |X_t| (t = 1, …, D),   I = S / D
where SPEI_t is the index value in month t of the event. Trends were tested with the Mann–Kendall test [@mann1945] and Sen's slope [@sen1968] after trend-free pre-whitening (TFPW) [@yue2002]. District means were the primary basis for inference, with Benjamini–Hochberg control of the false discovery rate (FDR) [@benjamini1995] across the five units and across the cells of each trend map [@wilks2016]. The index and time scale most related to losses were chosen by the mean correlation, over the five units, of each index ending in December–April with detrended official wheat yields.
## Hydrological drought and propagation
Gauge SRI and SDI were calibrated on 1985–2003, the part of the common baseline covered by the WAPDA records, and GloFAS SRI and SDI on 1985–2014. The propagation lag was the lag of maximum cross-correlation between the SPEI-k (k = 1–12) of each gauge's catchment and its SRI-1, at lags of 0–6 months, as in Sarwar et al. [@sarwar2022]. Dhok Pathan was paired with the Soan basin and Chirah with its own 353 km² catchment. The Sil gauge could not be located in the WAPDA records, so it was used for SRI and SDI only.
## Climate projections and model evaluation
We used NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 [@thrasher2022], a 0.25° statistically downscaled and bias-adjusted version of CMIP6 [@eyring2016], for the historical experiment and SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 (one run per model). Of the models with all six variables needed for Penman–Monteith ET0 (precipitation, Tmax, Tmin, relative humidity, shortwave radiation and wind), 27 were available for both scenarios. Each model was evaluated over 1985–2014 against CHIRPS (precipitation), ERA5-Land (Tmax, Tmin and ET0) and the stations, with the Kling–Gupta efficiency [@gupta2009]
@@EQ (6)|KGE = 1 − √[(r − 1)² + (α − 1)² + (β − 1)²]
where r is the correlation, α the ratio of standard deviations and β the ratio of means of the simulated and observed annual cycles over the domain, together with the spatial pattern correlation and the normalised root-mean-square difference. Models with a negative KGE for any variable were ranked last. The others were ranked by compromise programming [@zeleny1973] with entropy weights of the evaluation criteria (16, or 15 when no station series of a variable was eligible). To make the selection robust to the choice of years, we repeated the ranking in 1,000 moving-block bootstrap samples [@kunsch1989] of 1985–2014 (blocks of 5 years) and retained the ten models with the highest probability of a top-10 rank, weighted by the inverse of their compromise distance L.
## Bias correction and validation
Remaining biases were removed with quantile delta mapping (QDM) [@cannon2015], applied per 0.25° cell and calendar month with 1985–2014 as calibration period:
@@EQ (7)|x̂_p(t) = F_o⁻¹[ F_p,t(x_p(t)) ] × x_p(t) / F_h⁻¹[ F_p,t(x_p(t)) ]
for precipitation, radiation and wind (multiplicative), and the additive form x̂_p(t) = x_p(t) + F_o⁻¹(τ_t) − F_h⁻¹(τ_t) for temperature and humidity, where x_p(t) is the projected value at time t, τ_t its non-exceedance probability in the projected distribution F_p,t, and F_o and F_h the observed and historical cumulative distributions. QDM preserves the projected change in each quantile. It was validated with a plain and a detrended split sample (calibration 1985–1999, validation 2000–2014), odd/even-year cross-validation and a pseudo-reality test in which each model served in turn as the truth. Acceptance criteria were fixed in advance: the median monthly bias had to decrease for every variable, and no month could keep a precipitation bias above 25%. In three models (CNRM-CM6-1, CNRM-ESM2-1 and EC-Earth3-Veg-LR), the scenario wind of the source files dropped by about a third at 2015, and we rescaled it so that the scenario series continued the historical one.
## Projected changes and robustness
Changes were computed for the near (2025–2050), mid (2051–2075) and far (2076–2100) future relative to 1985–2014, and at global warming levels of +1.5, +2 and +3 °C, using each model's 20-year window around the year it crosses the level [@hauser2022]. Ensemble changes are skill-weighted means with the 10th–90th percentile model range. A change was robust when at least 80% of the models agreed on its sign and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test across models gave p < 0.05; on maps the p-values were adjusted for the false discovery rate across the cells of each map.
## Joint return periods
Drought events of the retained models were pooled for each period and warming level, with runs separated by a single non-drought month merged. The return period of an event that exceeds both a duration d and a severity s is
@@EQ (8)|T_AND = μ / [1 − F_D(d) − F_S(s) + C(F_D(d), F_S(s))]
where μ is the mean inter-arrival time of events and C the copula. Because durations are whole months, D − 1 was fitted with a discrete distribution (geometric, Poisson or negative binomial) and F_D was evaluated at d − 0.5, so that every estimator refers to the same events. Severity is at least 1 by construction; S − 1 was fitted with an empirical distribution up to its 90th percentile and a generalized Pareto tail above it [@coles2001]. The parametric copula was selected by the Akaike information criterion among eight families and tested with a parametric-bootstrap Cramér–von Mises test [@genest2009]. Because the parametric copula was rejected in most sets, the empirical Bernstein copula [@sancetta2004] was the primary estimator under a rule fixed in advance, with the parametric copula and a direct count of events as checks [@shiau2006]. Confidence intervals of 95% came from 1,000 bootstrap resamples of the models, and return periods longer than twice the pooled record length L were not reported. The reference events were the 10-, 25- and 50-year univariate duration and severity of the pooled baseline.
## Exposure of rainfed crops
Rainfed wheat and groundnut areas were taken from the un-irrigated area reported by the Crop Reporting Service for each district (mean of 2021–22 to 2023–24) and allocated within each district with SPAM2010 [@yu2020] and the ESA WorldCover 2021 cropland mask [@zanaga2022]. Graded exposure is the rainfed area weighted by the drought frequency of its cell, that is, the area expected to be in drought in an average month. We report its change relative to the baseline, the share of the area where drought frequency at least 1.5 times its baseline value, and, for each model, its own change, from which we derived the weighted model range and a 1,000-replicate bootstrap confidence interval.
## Uncertainty partition and sensitivity tests
The variance of decadal drought frequency was partitioned into internal variability, model and scenario uncertainty following Hawkins and Sutton [@hawkins2009]. Sensitivity tests replaced Penman–Monteith ET0 with Hargreaves, the threshold −1 with −1.5, and the 1985–2014 baseline with 1981–2010; repeated the projections without QDM, with QDM on pooled three-month windows and with TerraClimate targets; changed the model set; and compared a second downscaling chain (ISIMIP3b [@lange2019]) for the five models it shares with NEX-GDDP-CMIP6.
## Pre-registration and reproducibility
The rules for model selection, bias correction, return periods, wording of directional statements and FDR control were written down and frozen before the projections were computed. When the station inputs of the model ranking were corrected after a code review, the selection was frozen again before the corrected projections were run; both earlier selections are reported as sensitivity sets. The whole analysis is one R Markdown document with a configuration file, a package lock file and 100 unit tests, and every number in the Results is generated from the analysis outputs.
# Results
## Rainfall rose faster than evaporative demand over 1981–2024
Over the five units, mean annual precipitation in 1985–2014 was 786 mm in CHIRPS, 888 mm in CRU TS 4.10 and 1025 mm in ERA5-Land, and June–September contributed 64% of the CHIRPS total. Penman–Monteith ET0 from ERA5-Land inputs was 1384 mm yr⁻¹, against 1342 mm yr⁻¹ from TerraClimate inputs and 1444 mm yr⁻¹ from the Hargreaves method (Supplementary Fig. 1). At Jhelum, the one station with observed humidity and wind and a homogeneous ET0 series inside the domain, ERA5-Land ET0 correlated with station ET0 at r = 0.98 with a bias of +21%.
Averaged over the five units, annual precipitation increased by 56.4 mm per decade (p = 0.0009) and ET0 by 21.7 mm per decade (p = 0.016) (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 2). Tmax rose by 0.38 °C and Tmin by 0.34 °C per decade. The ET0 trend was therefore 39% of the rainfall trend, and the water balance P − ET0 showed no significant trend (+35.2 mm per decade, p = 0.13). December SPI-12 increased (+0.40 per decade, p = 0.001), whereas December SPEI-12 did not change significantly (p = 0.15). Precipitation increased in all five units (45–77 mm per decade), but the sign of the regional rainfall trend depended on the product: CHIRPS gave +65 mm per decade (p = 0.001), ERA5-Land −97 mm per decade (p = 0.001), and CRU TS and PERSIANN-CDR showed no significant change.
Run theory identified 35 SPEI-3 droughts in 1981–2024 (8.0 per decade), with a mean duration of 2.3 months and a mean severity of 3.22 (Fig. 3a). The most severe began in July 2009 (D = 7 months, S = 10.8), and the second in January 2002, during the long dry period of 1999–2002. Concurrent SPI-3 and SPEI-3 drought below −1.5 occurred in 16 months (3.0%).

## December SPEI-3 tracks rainfed wheat losses
Detrended official wheat yields correlated most strongly with SPEI-3 ending in December (mean r = 0.60), and the correlation was significant in all five units (Fig. 4c). SPEI-6 ending in March followed (r = 0.56). With the GDHY yields the same index gave r = 0.69 and also ranked first. SPEI-3 ending in December integrates rainfall and evaporative demand over October–December, the sowing and establishment period of rainfed wheat, and was therefore used for the drought characteristics, projections and return periods. For groundnut, pooled yield anomalies of four districts (27 district-seasons) correlated with SPEI-3 ending in September at r = 0.44 (p = 0.021).
The regional SPEI-3 agreed with SPEIbase v2.10 at r = 0.82 (RMSE 0.60), with 90% agreement in the classification of drought months, although SPEIbase uses CRU TS inputs at 0.5° and a different calibration period. It correlated with the cropland VHI at r = 0.62 and with standardised C3S/ESA CCI soil moisture at r = 0.55, higher than the SPI-3 (r = 0.47; Supplementary Table 3). At the station cells, CHIRPS SPI-3 correlated with station SPI-3 at r = 0.56, higher than ERA5-Land (r = 0.38). The drought bulletins were a weak check: the 49 monthly bulletins of 2020–2024 declared drought for the Potohar in 3 months, with a hit rate of 0.14 against SPEI-3 ≤ −1.
## Meteorological drought reaches the Soan within a month
SPEI-1 of the Soan basin correlated most strongly with gauged SRI-1 at Dhok Pathan at lag 0 (r = 0.61, 1981–2003; Fig. 4a, b). At Chirah, the maximum was at k = 2 months (r = 0.51). Drought in the Soan therefore follows rainfall deficits within the same month. GloFAS reproduced the lag (k = 1 month, lag 0) but with a weaker correlation (r = 0.59 over the gauge years and 0.50 over 1981–2024). Against the gauges, monthly GloFAS discharge reached a KGE of 0.59 at Dhok Pathan and 0.23 at Chirah, where it was 34% too high, and it detected 33% of the gauge SRI-3 drought months at both gauges (Supplementary Table 4). Annual flow at Chirah decreased after 1995 (Pettitt p = 0.044), whereas the shifts at Dhok Pathan (p = 0.397) and on the Sil (p = 0.134) were not significant.

## Ten models were retained by a resampled skill ranking
No model had a negative KGE for any variable (Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 5). The ten models with the highest probability of a top-10 rank were MIROC6, GISS-E2-1-G, NorESM2-MM, CNRM-CM6-1, ACCESS-ESM1-5, CMCC-ESM2, CNRM-ESM2-1, INM-CM4-8, NorESM2-LM and GFDL-CM4. MIROC6 was in the top 10 in 93% of the bootstrap samples, whereas the tenth model reached it in only 56%, so the selection is stable at the top and less so at the cut (Supplementary Table 6). The selection shared nine models with each of the selections under the other two criterion weightings.
The raw NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 wind was 4.1 times the station wind and 2.3 times ERA5-Land, and the shortwave radiation was 23% higher than ERA5-Land, so bias correction of all Penman–Monteith inputs was needed. QDM reduced the out-of-sample median bias of Tmax (from 2.00 to 1.30 °C), humidity, radiation and wind (from 107.5% to 4.1%) in the detrended split sample (Supplementary Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 7). For precipitation, QDM reduced the bias of the 90th percentile (from 75% to 29%) but not of the median (24.2% to 27.7%), so both pre-specified acceptance criteria failed for precipitation (Supplementary Table 8). As specified in advance, QDM was kept, and we quantified the consequence: QDM made the projected rainfall wetter (far-future change +15.7% and +31.5% against +6.8% and +19.3% in the published data, SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5) and thereby reduced the projected increase in drought frequency (from +11.5 and +14.6 to +10.0 and +11.1 percentage points). The primary result is therefore the more conservative one.

## Drought becomes more frequent late in the century despite more rain
Relative to the model baseline (17.5% of months in drought), SPEI-3 drought frequency did not change in 2025–2050 (−0.9 percentage points under both scenarios) and increased by 5.7 and 6.7 percentage points in 2051–2075, a change that was not robust (Table 1). By 2076–2100 it increased by 10.0 percentage points under SSP2-4.5 (10th–90th model range −0.9 to 20.9) and by 11.1 under SSP5-8.5 (0.2 to 23.4), and both changes were robust. After FDR control the increase was robust in 82% (SSP2-4.5) and 88% (SSP5-8.5) of the grid cells (Fig. 6) and in all five units (Supplementary Table 9). Mean severity increased by 1.52 and 1.79.
The increase came with more rain: annual precipitation increased by 15.7% and 31.5% and ET0 by 7.7% and 12.4% (SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5). The seasonal changes are consistent with this. Under SSP5-8.5, June–August precipitation increased by 43.0%, whereas December–February precipitation changed by only +1.7% while December–February ET0 increased by 20.4%. Drought frequency therefore increased in winter (December–February, +26.6 percentage points) and spring (March–May, +27.9), changed little in the monsoon (June–August, +2.5) and decreased in autumn (September–November, −12.7). For December SPEI-3, the index tied to wheat yields, the increase was 34.9 percentage points.
At global warming levels of +1.5, +2 and +3 °C the change in drought frequency was −0.9, +2.5 and +5.9 percentage points, none of them robust, while ET0 increased by 1.9%, 4.5% and 7.8% (Supplementary Fig. 4). The response at a given warming level was thus weaker and less consistent across models than the late-century response under SSP2-4.5.

## The baseline 25-year drought becomes a 1-in-10-year event
The pooled events of the ten models (202–348 per set) were fitted well by the marginal distributions: the duration and severity margins passed the parametric-bootstrap Anderson–Darling test in all ten sets. The parametric copula was rejected by the Cramér–von Mises test in all ten sets, so the Bernstein copula was the primary estimator (Table 2). The baseline 25-year event, a drought of at least 7 months with a severity of at least 9.2, had a return period of 50.1 years (95% CI 23.3 years to more than twice the record length) for the joint exceedance of both, against 43.4 years from the parametric copula and 33.3 years from a direct count. By 2076–2100 its return period shortened to 9.5 years (6.9–15.9) under SSP2-4.5 and to 10.0 years (7.2–18.4) under SSP5-8.5, and the three estimators agreed (8.1–10.0 years) (Fig. 7). At +1.5, +2 and +3 °C the return period was 28.5, 19.1 and 17.0 years.

## Most rainfed wheat faces more frequent drought
Of the 592 × 10³ ha of rainfed wheat, 104 × 10³ ha was in drought in an average month of the baseline. By 2076–2100 this exposure increased by 62% under SSP2-4.5 and by 71% under SSP5-8.5, and that of rainfed groundnut by 66% and 80% (Table 3, Fig. 8). Computed model by model, the increase for wheat had a 95% confidence interval of 31–93% under SSP2-4.5 and 39–104% under SSP5-8.5, and 84% and 93% of the model weight projected an increase (Supplementary Table 10). Under SSP5-8.5 the increase was largest in Chakwal (82%) and smallest in ICT (46%), and drought frequency reached at least 1.5 times its baseline value on 93% of the wheat area. The SPAM2010 rainfed wheat area was 2.3 times the area reported for 2011–2013, so scaling the crop areas to the official statistics was essential (Supplementary Table 11). By the binary measure, cells with a robust increase covered 86% (SSP2-4.5) and 94% (SSP5-8.5) of the rainfed wheat area (Supplementary Table 12).

## Model differences dominate the uncertainty
Model uncertainty was the largest source of variance in decadal drought frequency, at 71% in 2050 and 80% in 2095, while internal variability declined from 42% in 2030 to 19% in 2095 and scenario uncertainty was 1% (Fig. 9). The far-future increase stayed positive in every sensitivity test: 11.1 and 12.5 percentage points with Hargreaves ET0, 8.4 and 11.3 with the threshold −1.5, 8.9 and 10.1 with the 1981–2010 baseline, and 11.5 and 14.6 without QDM (Supplementary Table 13). Across seven model sets it ranged from 9.1 to 12.8 percentage points under SSP5-8.5, and it was robust in 12 of the 14 set and scenario combinations (Supplementary Table 14). A second downscaling chain, ISIMIP3b, gave 8.2 percentage points for its five models under SSP5-8.5, against 7.5 from NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 for the same models.

# Discussion
Drought on the Potohar Plateau is projected to become more frequent in the late century even though the region becomes wetter. Annual rainfall increases by 16–32% by 2076–2100, but the increase falls in the monsoon, whereas winter and spring rainfall barely changes while evaporative demand rises. Drought frequency therefore rises by 26–28 percentage points in winter and spring under SSP5-8.5, the season of rainfed wheat, and by 35 percentage points for December SPEI-3, the index most closely tied to wheat yields. The annual drought frequency rises by 10–11 percentage points under both scenarios. This is the divergence between precipitation-only and water-balance indices that Yang et al. [@yang2026] described globally, here concentrated in the cropping season. The seasonal pattern agrees with the wetter monsoon projected for the Potohar by Khan et al. [@khan2024potohar], although our ensemble shows winter rainfall nearly unchanged rather than lower. Because the SPI does not see evaporative demand, a drought assessment of the plateau based on the SPI alone would understate the risk to wheat.
The increase is robust in more than four-fifths of the grid cells and in all five units, and it survives every sensitivity test, a second downscaling chain and changes of the model set. The scenario contributes little to the uncertainty, and the two scenarios give similar late-century changes, so model differences, not the emission pathway, dominate the spread. The response at fixed global warming levels was weaker and not robust. Part of the late-century signal may therefore come from changes that are not proportional to global warming, such as the seasonal redistribution of rainfall, and the warming-level estimates should be read as a lower and more uncertain bound. The near-term period shows no change, which gives time for adaptation but also means that observed trends to date are not a guide to the late-century risk.
Our results extend earlier work on the plateau. Ain et al. [@ain2020] projected Kharif and Rabi drought on the Potwar Plateau with two regional models under RCP scenarios; with a CMIP6 ensemble selected for skill, we find the largest increase in winter and spring, the Rabi season. Goheer et al. [@goheer2023] mapped drought risk with satellite data; we add the link between the drought index and official yields, gauged discharge, and an exposure estimate with its uncertainty. As in the Soan basin studies [@abbas2021; @sarwar2022], meteorological drought propagated to river flow within one to two months. The quick response means that a winter–spring drought is likely to reduce river flow in the same season.
The joint return periods translate the frequency change into a planning quantity. A drought of at least seven months with a severity of at least 9.2, which recurred about once in 50 years in the baseline (33–50 years depending on the estimator), recurs about once in 10 years by 2076–2100 under both scenarios. The baseline value carries a wide uncertainty, because only nine pooled events exceeded the reference, whereas the far-future values agree across the three estimators. For rainfed wheat, the drought-weighted area grows by about two-thirds, with the largest increases in Chakwal and Attock, which together hold more than half of the rainfed wheat area. Drought-tolerant varieties, adjusted sowing dates and water harvesting for supplementary irrigation in these districts could be tested against this risk.
# Limitations of the study
The observational record is short and sparse. PMD daily records were available for only two stations, Islamabad and Murree, up to 2010, and they contain no humidity, wind or sunshine. After the homogeneity and representativeness screening, only Chaklala and Islamabad could be used to evaluate precipitation and only Jhelum and Peshawar to evaluate ET0. Tmax failed the homogeneity tests at every station, so the station criterion for Tmax was dropped from the model ranking. The direction of the observed rainfall trend also depended on the product.
The WAPDA gauge records end in 2003, so gauge SRI and SDI were calibrated on 1985–2003 rather than the full baseline, and hydrological drought after 2003 relies on GloFAS-ERA5, which matched the gauges only moderately at Dhok Pathan and poorly at Chirah. The location of the Sil gauge near Chahan is not recorded, so it was not used for propagation. The drought bulletins could only be recovered for 2012–2024 and were not a discriminating check.
QDM failed its pre-specified acceptance criteria for precipitation: it did not reduce the out-of-sample median bias, and it made the projected rainfall wetter. We kept it as specified in advance and showed that it reduces, rather than inflates, the projected drought increase. In three models a discontinuity in the source wind had to be corrected. The model selection was frozen again after a correction of its station inputs, at a time when earlier projections had been seen; the new selection shares nine of ten models with each earlier one, and the drought increase is similar for all of them. The parametric copula was rejected in every set, so the return periods rest on the empirical Bernstein copula, and the baseline return period is uncertain.
Groundnut statistics start in 2017–18, so groundnut served as a check rather than for index selection, and the crop allocation within districts relies on SPAM2010. A homogenized network of daily stations with humidity and wind, gauged discharge after 2003 and dynamically downscaled simulations would reduce these uncertainties.
# Conclusions
We projected drought over the rainfed Potohar Plateau with ten skill-selected, bias-corrected CMIP6 models, using the three-month SPEI ending in December, the index most closely tied to district wheat yields (r = 0.60). By 2076–2100, drought months become 10.0 (SSP2-4.5) and 11.1 (SSP5-8.5) percentage points more frequent, a robust change in all five units, even though annual rainfall increases by 16–32%. The increase is concentrated in winter and spring, when rainfall barely changes while evaporative demand rises, and therefore falls in the growing season of rainfed wheat.
A drought of at least seven months that recurred about once in 33–50 years in 1985–2014 recurs about once in 10 years by the end of the century, and the drought exposure of rainfed wheat grows by 62–71%, most in Chakwal and Attock. Meteorological drought reaches the Soan within a month. Model differences, not the emission scenario, dominate the uncertainty, and the increase holds in every sensitivity test. Drought planning for the plateau should therefore be based on an index that includes evaporative demand and should target the Rabi season.
